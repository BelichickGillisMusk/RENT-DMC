# Private solar demand a state can budget now

UNDEFEATED Solar · October 18, 2026

Question this note answers: if public buyers, systems integrators, and a state like California keep adding load — including robots and software used by state workers, and housing for renters and low-income households — what solar do they need from private sellers right now, while America.gov and military hiring stay slow? Compare that to a $475,000 retail tiltrotor sale. Say whether to wait on larger wafers, or to sell batteries with the canopy.

The $475,000 figure is the retail average given for this comparison. It is not a surveyed aircraft price.

## What to sell now

Sell the tracking canopy on lots a public buyer already owns: staff parking, campuses, fleet yards, water districts, federal lots. That is the current offer on the sales sheet (through 2028), with storage, managed charging, and site controls priced as later options.

A state that can budget does not need a new statewide solar program aimed at each renter, each welfare case, or each office robot. Those people do not buy the roof. The buyer is the agency, the housing owner, the campus, or the fleet. Size the system to that site’s bill, stalls, and critical loads.

California is already buying grid capacity. CPUC procurement orders include 2,000 MW of net qualifying capacity due in 2027, 2,000 MW in 2028, and 2,000 MW in each of 2030, 2031, and 2032. That capacity is reliability credit, not a solar nameplate order. CAISO’s 2025–2026 transmission plan approved 38 projects estimated at $6.7 billion. Berkeley Lab’s national sample of completed transmission-connected projects in 2025 took a median of more than five years from interconnection request to operation. The private opening is that wait. A canopy on an existing lot can be scoped while those projects are still in the queue.

Do not quote a statewide megawatt gap for “robots plus welfare plus state workers.” No public forecast gives that number. Berkeley Lab’s June 2026 update puts U.S. data-center electricity in a 2030 reference case of 649 TWh inside a 521–843 TWh range. That is a national data-center figure, not a California headcount of benefit recipients.

## Renters, low income, state workers, robots

Apartment renters and low-income households use power in buildings they do not own. The private sale is a canopy or rooftop on that building, or a housing-authority lot, contracted with the owner. A panel kit sold to the tenant does not match how the building is metered.

A state worker running software, or a robot on a route, is a plug load on a building or a yard that already has an electric bill. It raises the case for a site that already has daytime parking and a fleet. It does not create one solar plant per person.

Systems-integrator work follows the same sites: layout, chargers, monitoring, and a storage add when the outage or evening case is real. The October 12 research brief already treats statewide shortage-by-2028 language as unproven. The 2026 CERRO draft’s base case supports the reliability standard through 2031 under normal hydro and transmission. Use the site bill. Leave the shortage headline off the quote.

## One tiltrotor check, in solar units

Published 2025 California residential PV-plus-storage medians (NLR Winter 2026 Solar and Storage Industry Update, from California Distributed Generation Statistics):

- $4,597 per kWdc of PV
- $2,528 per kWh of battery
- $3,299 per kWac of battery

Battery power cost fell 42% from 2024 to 2025. Battery energy cost fell 18%. PV cost in these paired systems fell 12%. Median residential storage size in 2025 was 13.5 kWh. U.S. residential storage attachment rose from 25% of new PV systems in 2024 to 37% in 2025, with California among the highest.

If the entire $475,000 were spent on only one of those median component prices:

| Piece | What $475,000 covers at the 2025 median |
| --- | ---: |
| PV portion only | about 103 kWdc |
| Battery energy only | about 188 kWh |
| Battery power only | about 144 kWac |

Those rows are not additive. A real paired system includes PV, battery, structure, labor, and permits. The arithmetic shows the scale of one tiltrotor retail check: a small commercial canopy, not a power plant and not a purchase per household.

At the end of January 2026, NLR reported global modules around $0.10/Wdc and U.S. modules around $0.27/Wdc. On 100 kWdc, the U.S. module line is about $27,000. Most of a $475,000 installed sale is steel, electrical gear, labor, and permits. Soft costs and balance-of-system were about 80% of 2025 median installed residential and non-residential prices in the Berkeley Lab distributed-solar update.

Jobs in that check are install, electrical, and service crews on lots the buyer already operates. That is work that can be bid while longer America.gov and military pipelines move. This note does not assign a headcount.

## Wafers in the next year or two

Larger wafers are already the commodity. DOE’s supply-chain record is the shift to 182 mm M10 and 210 mm G12. A 210 mm square has about 33% more area than a 182 mm square. That is fewer modules for the same watts if efficiency is the same. It is not 33% more sunlight turned into electricity, and it is not a 33% cut in the installed price.

Global wafer, cell, and polysilicon prices rose 30–44% in 2025. Waiting a year or two for a bigger wafer does not get ahead of a product the canopy can already specify. Perovskite tandem cells are a lab-to-pilot story. They are not a reason to hold 2026–2028 public canopy sales. When a larger module is worth buying, qualify it on dimensions, weight, current, and the tracker’s mounting rules. The October 12 brief already says to skip a new wafer factory.

## Batteries

Batteries are the nearer upgrade. A canopy without storage covers daytime use and shade. Evening charging, peak windows, and a defined outage load need storage and engineered islanding. The sales sheet already lists storage as a priced option after the base system, not as a substitute for it.

California’s own 2025 prices moved fastest on the battery power rating ($/kWac down 42%). Residential attachment is already high. Non-residential attachment is lower (11% of U.S. installs in 2025), so a public lot will not show up with a battery unless the quote includes it.

Use a battery when the site has a departure-time charging schedule, a peak bill, or a critical load that has to ride through an outage. Skip it when the buyer only needs shade and daytime offset. Do not wait on wafer news to make that call.
